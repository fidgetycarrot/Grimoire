# Grimoire

**The story behind your chat: where it is going, why, and how fast.**

Grimoire is a Lumiverse extension that gives the narrator a story of its own. It keeps a private notebook for each chat: what is really going on, what people want, which threads are open, and what happens if you leave them alone. Before each reply it gives the narrator a short private note from that notebook. Every so often it checks the notebook against the chat and updates it.

It works with any card: downloaded, homemade, or made with Set Points. It never puts prewritten text into your chat. The narrator writes every word; Grimoire only steers.

## Who it is for

Grimoire is for roleplayers who like to play the scene in front of them, without having to think about the whole story. You react to what happens; Grimoire remembers that the piece of paper you picked up matters later, that someone across town wants something, and when it's time for things to move. You never have to manage it. When you do want a say, you can give it one, and that's always optional.

Grimoire tries to make a roleplay feel like a good story, with real pacing, threads that pay off, and a world that makes sense. But it is still a roleplaying tool, not a book or a movie. Fun comes first, and a cohesive, well-paced story comes close behind: over a long chat, that's much of what keeps it fun. If a scene goes somewhere sooner than a novel would, that's fine; Grimoire's job is to keep the story holding together and moving at a good pace around it.

New features should keep these promises: they make play more fun, they keep the story cohesive and well paced, and they work without your attention. Anything that asks for your input is something you choose to use.

## What it does

- **Writes a notebook from your card.** It reads the card, your persona, the card's and the chat's lorebooks, and anything already said, then plans the story: the central question, the hidden truth, a small supporting cast with their own goals, a few threads, and moments the story could reach.
- **Grimoire leads the story.** You can just play the scene in front of you and be carried along. Every part of the story has one **main storyline**: the story you're in, with side storylines around it. Each part opens with its inciting moment right away (a job, a problem that lands, someone who needs something; in a kishōtenketsu part, an invitation or a question worth following), so you start out on a story instead of working out how you fit. The note tells the narrator the main storyline's next step is what the story does now, not something to slip in if it fits, and if you don't go to it, it comes to you: someone tied to it seeks you out, word reaches you, or it happens where you are. You still choose what your character does. For the first replies of a story (about 30 at the default wait) and of each new chapter (about 15), the main storyline moves at a brisker pace and side matters stay small. The usual rules still hold: big moments are built up to, and only one storyline is urgent at a time.
- **Works with narrator cards.** A card that's a narrator, a game master, or a world (or one left nearly blank so a setting lorebook carries the world) is treated as the voice that runs the story, not a person in it. Grimoire builds a slightly bigger cast from the lore, your ideas, and your persona's world instead.
- **Choose how the story is built.** When you write a notebook, pick **Conflict** (a problem that builds to a climax), **Kishōtenketsu** (people and discovery, with a turn instead of a fight), or let Grimoire choose. Grimoire may switch shapes between parts as the story goes, unless you tick **Keep this shape for the whole chat**. You can change that later under **More**.
- **Uses Lumiverse's memory.** Grimoire reads what Lumiverse's Memory Cortex remembers about the chat (past moments, people and places with what's known about them, how people stand with each other) when it writes a notebook, at check-ups, new chapters, and when you talk to it, so plans honor old grudges, debts, promises, and losses. It also adds the memories that fit the scene to the note, so any preset has them, even one without the `{{memories}}` block; when your preset already includes them, the note leaves them out on its own. Settings → **Add Lumiverse's memories to the note** turns the note part off. **Behind the scenes** shows how much Lumiverse remembers for the chat and what the last reply got, so you can see whether the memory is keeping up. Grimoire only reads the memory, never changes it.
- **Big setting lorebooks.** A lorebook with a whole world in it (cities, factions, hundreds of entries) is read the smart way rather than first-come. Grimoire gets the always-on entries, the full text of the entries that matter right now, and an index of everything else, grouped by chapter ("Brackport: Walkways; Docks; …"). So it knows the whole world exists, builds from its places and people instead of inventing its own, and can plan toward parts you haven't seen yet. "Matter right now" means the entries the narrator has in play, then the ones closest in meaning to the recent chat and to each open storyline, found with your own embedding connection (Settings → Embeddings in Lumiverse). That works whether or not the entries are vectorized for the narrator, and finds entries with no keywords at all, like how debts or rumors work. Each entry is embedded once and kept in memory while Lumiverse runs; only changed entries are done again. Without embeddings, entries are found by their keywords and chapter names instead. The first notebook reads widely, up to about 40,000 tokens of lore, most relevant first; check-ups and talk read about 8,000. The lorebook is canon: Grimoire invents only small things, and when you go after a mystery, the answer is earned over time and fits everything the lore says. Lore is read from the card's lorebooks and the chat's, not global ones.
- **Works on old chats too.** A long chat is read from the very first message, in parts, into a record of the story so far. The notebook then picks up the threads and people the chat already has, and plans what comes next from there. The panel tells you how many parts it will read before you start.
- **Paced by story time and by your play.** Threads move at their own speed (urgent, steady, or slow burn). Story time keeps it plausible: ignore the stolen ring for one evening and nothing happens; leave it for days and the search reaches you. But a roleplay can spend a hundred replies in one story day, so a thread also moves on after a number of replies (Settings → Longest a story thread waits), whichever comes first. The other way round holds too: steps planned an hour apart in story time still wait at least a third of that many replies after the last one (about 5 for a steady storyline at the default), so a problem can't race to its end in a few messages while the clock barely moves. An opening moment and the "Story feeling stuck?" buttons still arrive at once. Warning signs come before consequences, and big scenes aren't interrupted for slow threads.
- **Pacing that stays on plan.** Three rules keep the plan from speeding up on its own. Only one storyline is urgent at a time: a second emergency is sent back to be rethought as something that builds (prepared with a breadcrumb or two), and if it comes back anyway, code slows it and spaces its steps out. An existing storyline only gets faster, or has its next steps pulled closer together, when something you did made it more pressing, and the check-up has to say what. Each part of the story has a rough length (about 60 replies at the default wait, half that on Fast), so the climax waits for the last part of it and new storylines fit the time that's left. When the narrator keeps running ahead (steps happening before they were offered, or the quick check seeing it hurry past things), Grimoire re-plans the rest of the part's timing, at most once in a while. Your Fast pace still means faster: shorter waits and shorter parts.
- **Intimate scenes play out undisturbed.** With the quick check on, Grimoire notices when an intimate scene is under way. Until it ends, the narrator note says to let it play out at its own pace, and nothing planned interrupts it: no developments, no planned moments, no breadcrumbs, no time-skip suggestion, even on Fast. Its replies don't count toward the replies that move storylines along, so nothing piles up to land all at once afterward. Prefer the odd surprise? Settings → During intimate scenes → Let urgent things interrupt.
- **Everyone gets their own voice.** Each person in the notebook has a voice: how long their sentences run, the words they reach for or avoid, their habits, and a sample line. Whenever someone is in the scene, their voice goes into the note with the reminder that nobody should sound like anyone else, which pushes back on the one quippy voice narrators slide into. Check-ups keep each voice true to how the person has actually talked, fill in missing ones, and sharpen any two that have started to sound alike.
- **People with depth, not stock types.** Every person gets more than their role: a contradiction (a want that pulls against a fear or a habit), one specific detail that isn't the obvious one for their role, and a tie to the world. The two to four who matter most get a **sheet**: what a good character card would say about them (personality, history, habits, relationships, how they treat you, what they're like when pushed). The sheet goes into the note whenever they're in the scene, so the narrator plays them with depth instead of making them up on the spot, and check-ups keep it current as they change. Sheets hold no secrets and no looks (LumiCast keeps how people look). Describe someone in the ideas box ("I'd like to meet Tamsin, a retired smuggler who…") and they keep your words exactly; Grimoire builds on them and never changes them.
- **People with backbone.** Every note also says people hold their ground: pleading, charm, tears, flirting, or a good speech earn no automatic yes, and closeness doesn't make anyone a pushover. Grimoire builds each important person before naming them: a trait and a counter-trait, how they are with others, what irritates them, three specific hobbies, one thing they're proud of and one regret, and a small thing they never tell anyone. Names come from the setting, never the ones AI stories overuse. Every planned step names who acts and how you'd notice it; vague plans ("tension rises") are sent back. When a storyline goes against you, it leaves a mark that keeps showing ("still felt from earlier") and can grow a worse follow-up later.
- **Conflict that holds.** Narrators love to fix your trouble on the spot. Every note tells the narrator that problems take time: nothing is solved in the scene it comes up in, solutions cost something and can fail, and nobody produces the money, the answer, or the rescue out of nowhere. In a conflict part, the main storyline has an opponent: someone whose want collides with yours, who acts between scenes and pushes harder when ignored. With the quick check on, a new problem you raise (a debt, a threat) asks for a check-up that turns it into a storyline, and a problem the narrator solved too easily gets a catch, a cost, a delay, or someone it angers, without undoing what happened.
- **Two story shapes.** Each part of the story is either a **conflict** arc (setup, rising action, climax, resolution) or a **kishōtenketsu** arc (introduction, development, a turn that casts what came before in a new light, and bringing it together), with no conflict needed. The model picks the shape that fits: danger and mystery get conflict; romance, comfort, and everyday life get kishōtenketsu. The narrator's guidance follows the stage: during development it deepens instead of escalating.
- **Breadcrumbs.** The notebook keeps ordinary-looking details (an object, a habit, a stray remark) that the narrator mentions in passing, one at a time and spaced out, and brings back much later with their meaning: when a related development arrives, at the turn, or in a quiet moment. The narrator only learns what a detail means when it's time to pay it off, so it can't give it away early, and a kishōtenketsu turn waits until the details that prepare it have been planted. A breadcrumb whose story you leave behind isn't thrown away: it plays out offscreen as an **echo**, and later reaches you in passing, as a news clip, a rumor, or a remark.
- **Open-ended.** There's no length to pick. Grimoire plans the first part of the story; when its threads wrap up, a check-up starts the next part from what actually happened. Code checks this: a check-up that leaves nothing left to happen (only slow storylines, or none) is sent back to open the next part, and if it still doesn't, the panel says the part has wrapped up and highlights **Start a new chapter**.
- **Pace you can set.** One button above the message box switches between:
  - **Auto**: Grimoire reads the story's rhythm. Urgent things can interrupt; slower ones wait for a natural break. After a big moment it leaves room to breathe; when things stall, it lets an existing thread move.
  - **Quiet**: no new trouble starts. Anything already urgent still plays out, and the world keeps moving offscreen. It is room, not a pause button.
  - **Fast**: tight scenes, quicker consequences, short time skips.
- **Time-skip suggestions.** When a calm scene has gone quiet for a while and nothing is due, a small **Skip ahead?** button appears above the message box. Grimoire knows when the next planned development comes due, so it offers to skip to right then ("about 6 hours"), without saying what it is, plus a plain choice like "to this evening". It never skips on its own, never appears during big scenes, and **Not now** sends it away for a while.
- **Check-ups.** A check-up reads the recent chat next to the notebook. It marks what actually happened, moves clocks, adds people and threads you started, and redraws upcoming moments if you went somewhere else. It changes how the story gets somewhere, not why. Check-ups run when the scene changes, when time jumps, after a set number of replies, two replies after an urgent development was offered, or when you choose **Check now**. They read the chat as words: preset styling and panels are stripped, but facts in them are kept.
- **Keeps up when you change direction.** With the quick check on, every reply is also checked for a change of direction (you left the setting for good, walked away from the main situation, changed what you're after) and for planned developments that can no longer reach you. Those are held back at once, so the narrator isn't nudged toward a story you left. Then a check-up reworks the notebook: every open thread follows you, waits, comes after you, or ends with a reason; breadcrumbs are kept and re-aimed; the people you left keep living their lives. Nothing already set up is forgotten.
- **Reads your preset's tracker, whatever it is.** If your preset writes a status block (time, place, who is here), Grimoire learns where those lines live and reads them with fixed code. A field is trusted only after it has been read from a real reply. Common layouts (facts in tag attributes like `<scene_header location="…" time="…">`, or labeled lines like `Time:` and `Location:`) are found by Grimoire itself, for free, as soon as a reply has them. Anything else is described by the model when the notebook is written, or at the first check-up if the tracker only appears once the narrator starts replying (presets often leave it out of the greeting). Anything the tracker supplies is never asked for twice: if it keeps the time, Grimoire's clock reads from it. Saved chat variables can be read too.
- **Follows edits, swipes, and branches.** Each saved notebook version is tied to the exact messages it describes. Edit, delete, or swipe a reply and Grimoire falls back to the version from before it; nothing from the removed replies counts, and no extra check-up is needed. Only an edit to a message the first notebook was written from asks for one. Swipe back and the newer version returns. Forked chats start with a copy.
- **Gives way to your swipe guidance.** When you swipe with guidance, that reply's note steps back: it keeps story time, who people are, and what the story is about, but leaves out pace, plans, and planned moments, and tells the narrator your guidance wins. Your guidance is used for that reply only; Grimoire doesn't store it.
- **Your ideas from the start.** When you write a notebook, an optional box asks what you'd like in the story ("a slow-burn romance, trouble with old friends"). Grimoire builds the first plan around it and paces it in over time; tick **Keep these as lasting notes** to pin them. The box holds up to 3,000 characters, so there's room to describe a person or two.
- **Talk to Grimoire.** Not happy with where the story is going? Tell Grimoire in the panel: "this is moving too fast", "give the side characters more to do". It changes its plans and answers in a sentence or two, with no spoilers; what changed is listed only as shapes and counts ("Slowed 1 storyline", "Set aside 1 planned moment"). When a request is open-ended ("add some conflict", "I'm bored"), it first asks what kind you mean, offering two or three concrete options plus "something else"; specific requests ("slow down", "more of Sam") it simply acts on. It asks at most twice, and never keeps the conversation going for its own sake. Tick **Keep this as a lasting note** to pin your wish: pinned notes steer every check-up and every reply until you remove them. **Undo the last change** puts the plans back, as long as the story hasn't moved on since. While it thinks, the conversation shows a running clock and a **Stop** button (stopping changes nothing). To stay quick, its answers list only what changes rather than rewriting the whole notebook. Share an idea mid-story ("I'd love a road trip someday") and it's woven in the same way. Ask for ideas ("any ideas for Sam?") and it offers two or three short, open directions built on what you've seen, never its own secrets or plans; pick one or riff on it. Each message is one model request.
- **Out-of-character talk stays out of the story.** A message that starts with `[ooc]`, `(ooc)`, `OOC:`, `((`, or `//` is talk with the narrator, not story. It gets no story note (so nothing from the notebook can leak into the answer), no quick check, and neither it nor the answer counts toward the replies that move storylines along. An aside inside a story message, like `I wave. (ooc: make her warmer)`, keeps the story part as usual; check-ups read the aside as a wish, never as something that happened. If you write "[ooc]: continue the story" and the reply carries your preset's tracker, Grimoire knows the story went on.
- **Secrets that came out stay out.** Once the chat shows you've learned a hidden truth, a check-up moves it to the notebook's "came out" list, with the reply it came out at. From then on it's an established fact: people stop acting as if it were secret, no more clues or reveals are planned for it, and the story builds on it instead. With the quick check on, a secret coming out asks for that check-up right away. It all happens in the background; only the spoiler fold shows it.
- **Story feeling stuck?** Under Talk to Grimoire, four buttons get a stalled story moving: **Someone makes a move**, **A secret starts to surface**, **Something catches up with me**, or **Surprise me**. Grimoire picks something already in your story (a person and what they want, a storyline, a planted detail, something that happened, a secret) and sets it in motion. Code checks the tie: a push that doesn't grow out of something already in the notebook is sent back, and so is a new storyline with nobody you know in it. It starts with a small sign and builds over the next few replies, and you're told nothing about what it is. **Start a new chapter** (at the top of the panel, under Pace) wraps up what has run its course and starts the next part from where things stand, sometimes after a jump in time. Telling Grimoire you're bored in the talk box does the same. Either one can be undone until the next reply. One model request each.
- **Quick check (optional).** A small decision model reads each new reply and answers a few multiple-choice questions: did a planned development happen, did a possible moment happen, how intense is the scene, did something new come up. The notebook stays current between check-ups, the note stops offering things that already happened, and a full check-up runs as soon as something new needs planning. It uses Lumiverse's decision connections, so any decision model works (OpenRouter or TypeSafe, including Jev). **Quick check answers · no spoilers** shows its recent answers as numbers with plain labels ("Something new: 82%", "Scene: big moment (70%)") and what each led to, so you can see how jumpy it is without seeing any plans.
- **Spoilers stay hidden.** The panel shows the story time, the pace, and a one-line update with no spoilers. **At a glance · no spoilers** shows only shapes and counts: the story's shape and stage, how many threads are open and how fast, when the next development comes at the latest, and how many breadcrumbs are planted or paid off. The full notebook sits behind **Show the notebook · spoilers**, where you can read it or edit it as text.

## Your stories

Chats that have a notebook are listed on the home page under **Your stories**, newest first, with when you last played. Tap one to open it. The Grimoire drawer shows the same list when no chat is open. Turn the home page list off in **Settings → Show my stories on the home page**.

## Install

Requires **Lumiverse 1.2.0 or newer**. The built files are included; no build step is needed.

1. Open Lumiverse's **Extensions** panel and install from this repository: `https://github.com/fidgetycarrot/Grimoire`
2. Enable **Grimoire** and grant its permissions.
3. Open the **Grimoire** drawer tab, go to **Settings**, and choose a model connection.
4. Open a chat, choose **Write the notebook**, and play.

| Permission | Used for |
| --- | --- |
| `generation` | Writing notebooks and running check-ups on the connection you choose |
| `interceptor` | Adding the short private note before each reply |
| `chats` | Finding the chat and its character |
| `chat_mutation` | Reading the chat's messages so the notebook matches them. Lumiverse puts message reading under this permission; Grimoire never changes, adds, or deletes messages. |
| `characters` | Reading the card |
| `personas` | Reading your active persona (optional) |
| `world_books` | Reading the card's and the chat's lorebooks (optional) |
| `embeddings` | Finding the lore that fits the story by meaning, with your own embedding connection (optional; without it, lore is found by keywords and chapter names) |
| `memories` | Reading what Lumiverse's Memory Cortex remembers about the chat (optional; Grimoire never changes it) |
| `decisions` | The quick check, on your decision connection (optional) |

## Quick check

Needs a Lumiverse with decision connections (spindle types 0.6.45+, host capability `decisions-v1`).

1. In Lumiverse, add a decision connection (OpenRouter or TypeSafe) and pick a model. Mark one as default if you like.
2. In **Grimoire → Settings → Quick check after each reply**, turn it on. Leave the connection on your default or choose one. The model field is optional: type any decision model ID to override the connection's model.

Only confident answers change anything (70% or more for yes-or-no questions), and they only move the story forward: a quick check never un-marks anything. Its marks are provisional: the next full check-up confirms or corrects each one, so the smarter model always has the final say. Answers about a reply that was swiped or edited meanwhile are thrown away. Quick-check updates are tied to the reply they judged, so regenerating that reply goes back to the notebook from before it.

## Where the note goes

The note is added for you, just before the reply (any prefill is kept last). Prompt Breakdown labels it **Grimoire**. Two macros let your preset show a little more:

- `{{grimoire_clock}}`: the current story time.
- `{{grimoire_pace}}`: the current pace line.

## Costs

- Writing a notebook: one larger model request. A long chat adds one request per part of its earlier history (at most 12, shown before you start).
- Each check-up: one request. With a big lorebook, check-ups and talk carry up to about 8,000 more tokens of lore (writing the notebook, up to about 40,000, once).
- Ordinary replies: no extra request. The note adds a few hundred tokens to the prompt, and up to about 2,000 more while people with sheets are in the scene.
- With the quick check on: one small decision request per reply (a few questions, answers only). It replaces some check-ups, since those then run only when needed or after the set number of replies.

Small slips in the model's reply (an id with capitals, a field a little too long, one event too many, a stray tag) are tidied rather than rejected, and a check-up can't drop people or threads or undo what happened: anything it leaves out is put back. If a reply still can't be used, the panel says which field failed, without naming anything from the story.

If a check-up fails, the automatic ones wait for the story to move on a little before trying again. **Check now** always works.

## Limits in this version

- One character per chat. Group chats are not supported yet.
- Grimoire does not ask the narrator to write hidden updates. Between check-ups, a clock that Grimoire keeps itself can lag behind the chat; the note says so, and the chat wins. A preset tracker that records time avoids this.
- Tracker reading supports labeled lines, tags, tag attributes (like `<scene_header location="…">`), JSON in a tag or code block, and saved chat variables. Freely written status prose is still passed to check-ups as text.
- Editing a reply before the notebook was written keeps the first version and asks for a check-up.
- A time skip moves the parts of the clock Grimoire owns. If your tracker owns the time, the narrator is told about the skip and the tracker records it.
- The narrator can still ignore guidance or let a secret slip. The notebook makes the plan clear, current, and cheap to follow; it cannot force it.

```sh
npm install
npm run check
bun test
npm run build
```
